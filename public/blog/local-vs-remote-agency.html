Does your web design and SEO agency need to be in your town?

For the website and the SEO, it doesn't need to be. That work happens inside your website, your Google Business Profile, Search Console and your ad accounts. The Google accounts can all be shared with an agency from anywhere, without handing over your password. What does matter is whether the people doing the work understand your area and your customers, and you can test that whatever their address is.

Here's how to tell what genuinely needs someone nearby, and what to check before you hire either way.

Your agency's address doesn't decide where you show on the map

If the agency is in Gauteng and you're in George or Polokwane, Google won't treat your business as if it's in Gauteng. Google says its local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence, and distance means how far your business is from the person searching. The agency's location doesn't come into it.

Prominence covers how well-known your business is, and Google lists links to your business and your reviews among the things it looks at. Those are earned in your own area, by your own work and your own customers. The same page is also blunt that there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

So what helps you in your own town is a complete, accurate Business Profile, a website with a proper page for each service and the areas you really cover, and a steady trickle of genuine reviews. Our guide to local SEO for service businesses goes through each of those. None of it requires the agency to drive past your premises.

The accounts that matter can be shared from anywhere, and should stay yours

The main Google accounts an agency works in each have a way to let them in under their own name:

  1. Google Business Profile. As the owner, you can invite someone as a manager. A manager can edit your details, reply to reviews and publish posts, but can't add or remove other people or delete the profile. Google points out that each person uses their own Google Account, so nobody needs your password.
  2. Search Console. Only an owner can add users, with full or restricted access, and take that access away again.
  3. Google Ads. You invite a user by email and choose their access level, and you can change or remove it later.

That doubles as a good check on any agency, local or not. You stay the owner, and they work inside your accounts with their own login. If an agency wants your password, or wants to set the accounts up in its own name, ask why before anything else.

What genuinely benefits from knowing your area

Local knowledge shows up in the words on your site. When a page names the suburbs and towns the way your customers say them, talks about the problems they actually have, and prices things the way they expect, it reads as written by someone who knows the place. Generic copy written from a template somewhere else reads as imported.

An agency picks that up from you and from the searches your customers type, so ask how they'll learn it. A good brief covers the areas you serve and the ones you don't want, the jobs you want more of, and what your customers usually ask on the phone. If an agency can't tell you where that information goes on your site, its office location won't fix it.

Some owners simply prefer to sit across a table from the people they pay. That's a fair preference, and if it matters to you, ask about it before you sign. The other things that need someone on the ground, like photos of your vans, your team and finished jobs, come from you or a local photographer, whoever builds the site.

What to ask, whoever you hire

  1. Who owns the domain, the Business Profile, Search Console and the ad accounts? The answer should be you.
  2. Who will I actually talk to, and are they the person doing the work?
  3. How will I see progress between meetings, and in what form?
  4. How do I give feedback on the new site before it goes live?
  5. Which of my areas and services will get their own page, and how will you learn how my customers search?
  6. How do I leave, what happens to the website itself, and what do I take with me?

An agency down the road and an agency in another province should both answer these clearly and in writing.
