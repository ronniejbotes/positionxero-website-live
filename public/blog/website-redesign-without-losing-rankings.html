How do you rebuild your website without losing your Google rankings?

Keep the page addresses that already earn visits. Where an address has to change, set up a permanent redirect from it to the page that replaces it, and make sure the new page still says what the old one said. Google's own guide to moving a site with URL changes works the same way: map every old address to its new one, redirect, update your links and sitemap, then watch how the move goes.

So start before anyone opens the design file. Here's what to do, in order.

List the pages Google already sends people to

Before anything changes, find out which pages are doing the work. If you haven't got Search Console set up, do that first; our guide on getting your business on Google walks through it. In Google Search Console, the Performance report shows clicks and impressions by page and by search, and you can export the table. Open the Pages tab and save the export somewhere it won't get lost. Then click into your busiest pages and note the searches each one is shown for.

That list is your inventory. Next to each address, write what happens to it on the new site:

  1. Keep it at the same address.
  2. Move it to a new address, and note which.
  3. Merge it into another page, and note which.
  4. Drop it, because nobody needs it any more.

The pages that bring visits are the ones to protect hardest. On a service business site you'd expect that to be the homepage and the service pages, but the export is the judge: sometimes it's a blog post that turned out to answer a question people really ask.

Redirect every address that changes, straight to its new home

When an address changes, the old address should answer with a permanent redirect to the new page. Google recommends a permanent server-side redirect whenever possible: it treats a permanent redirect as a sign that the new address should be shown in results, and a temporary redirect as no such sign.

Google's site-move guide adds the details that trip people up. Redirect straight to the final address rather than through a chain of hops. Don't point a pile of old addresses at the new homepage because it's easier; Google says that can confuse visitors and may be treated as a soft "not found" error. If you've merged old pages into a new page, redirecting them all to that merged page is fine. And a page you've genuinely dropped should tell Google it's gone, with a proper "not found" or "gone" response.

The good news is in the same guide: "Don't worry about link credit." Google says permanent redirects don't cause a loss in PageRank, so the value other sites passed to the old page by linking to it carries across.

Leave the redirects in place long after launch. Google's site-move guide states how long it wants them kept.

Carry the words across, as well as the look

What a page says is part of how Google matches it to a search. Google reads the text and key tags of each page, such as its title, to work out what the page is about. Say your plumbing page is shown for "geyser replacement" because it explains geyser replacement properly, and the new version swaps that section for a slogan and a stock photo. The address can be perfect and you've still given Google less reason to show the page for that search.

Use the searches you noted in the inventory. For each page you're keeping or moving, check that the new version still answers those searches at least as well as the old version did: the page title, the main heading, the subheadings, and the plain explanation underneath. Rewrite freely, but rewrite with that list open.

Merging pages needs the same care. The merged page should cover what each of the old pages covered, or the searches that went to the old pages have nowhere to land.

Make sure the new site isn't hiding from Google on launch day

While a new site is being built, it may be hidden from Google on purpose, with a "noindex" tag on the pages or a block in the robots.txt file. That's sensible while the work is going on. The trouble is forgetting to take it off. Google's guide calls it out directly in its site-move checklist: remove any noindex or robots.txt blocks that were only needed for the move. The guide suggests making the list of pages to un-hide before launch, so un-hiding them becomes a checklist item on launch day.

On the same launch-day list:

  1. Every new page carries a canonical tag pointing at its own address.
  2. Links between your own pages use the new addresses, so visitors and Google aren't bounced through redirects to get around your site.
  3. A new sitemap lists the new addresses.
  4. If your domain itself is changing, Search Console's Change of Address tool comes into play. Google's guide says you don't need it when the domain stays the same and only the paths change.

Then click through the old addresses from your inventory and watch where they land.

After launch: submit the sitemap and watch Search Console

Submit the new sitemap in Search Console, then keep an eye on the Pages and Performance reports. Old addresses showing up as redirects is expected. Pages from your inventory showing as not indexed, or searches you used to appear for vanishing entirely, are what to chase.

Expect some movement. Google's guide says to expect temporary fluctuation in ranking while it recrawls and reindexes a site after a move. If a page drops away and stays away, go back through this list: is its redirect in place, did its content survive the rewrite, and is a noindex tag still sitting on it?

Whoever rebuilds it, ask for the list

Whoever rebuilds your site, us included, ask to see the list of old addresses and where each one goes before the new site goes live. If nobody can show you that list, nobody has checked where your current visitors will land.
