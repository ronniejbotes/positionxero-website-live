Do I need a website if I already have a Facebook page?

If your customers already know you, your Facebook page may be enough for now. If you want people who've never heard of you to find you on Google and call, you'll want a website as well, and the page keeps doing its job next to it.

Here's how to tell which of those describes your business, what each does well, and what you can check on your own page today.

What your Facebook page is good at

It's quick to start. Anyone with a Facebook account can create a Page or help run it. It's where you post photos of finished jobs, answer messages, and let people know when you're booked up. Your customers can share it in the neighbourhood group with a tap.

For a business whose work comes from neighbours, repeat customers and word of mouth, that can carry you a long way. If you're already turning work away, and the people who call you found you by name, a Facebook page on its own is a fair choice. There's no prize for having a website you don't need yet.

The trouble starts when you want work from strangers.

Why the page is never quite yours

Your Facebook page lives on Meta's property, under Meta's rules. Its terms say that if it decides, in its discretion, that you've clearly, seriously or repeatedly breached its terms or policies, it may suspend or permanently disable your access, or disable or delete the account. Meta says it will usually tell you and explain any options for a review, but the decision is Meta's.

You may never get near that. The more ordinary problem is access. The page is run from people's Facebook accounts, so ask yourself who set yours up. If it was a nephew, a past employee or the person who did your logo, and they've lost their password or moved on, you may find you can't change your own phone number on your own page.

Then there's who sees your posts. Facebook's Feed weighs signals about each post and each person, predicts what that person will find most interesting, and puts posts in order by a relevance score. Your post saying you have a gap on Thursday competes with friends' holiday photos, groups and videos, and Meta's system decides where it lands.

A website works differently. It sits on your own address, the words say what you want them to, and with the domain registered in your name, you can move it to someone else if you ever need to.

What a website does that a Facebook page can't

Think about how a stranger finds a plumber in Centurion. Their geyser bursts, they pick up their phone, and they type "plumber Centurion" or "geyser burst Lyttelton" into Google. They aren't searching Facebook, and they don't know your name.

Google says its local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity. Relevance, in Google's words, is how well your Business Profile matches what was searched. Popularity, which Google's page explains under the heading 'Prominence', is how well-known a business is, and Google says it draws on things like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have.

In our view, a website gives Google more of your business to match. You can have a page about geyser replacements, a page about blocked drains, and a page that names the suburbs you actually cover, each answering what a customer types. On Facebook you get the layout Facebook gives every Page, with your posts sliding down the timeline as you add new ones.

A website also does a quieter job: it reassures. When someone finds you through a friend's recommendation, they may look you up before they call. A site with your name, the areas you work in, photos of real jobs and a number that works tells them you're a real business that's still trading.

Use them together

You don't have to choose. Your Google Business Profile is the piece that ties them together: Google lets you share your website on your Business Profile and add your Facebook page as a social profile. Your website links to your Facebook page, your page links back to your website, and you keep posting on Facebook as you do now.

If you haven't set up a Business Profile yet, our guide on how to get your business on Google walks through it, along with getting a website indexed.

Checks you can run on your phone today

You can do these on your phone now, and they'll tell you more than any agency's opinion:

  1. Search Google for your trade and your suburb, the way a customer would. Look at who shows up, and whether you do.
  2. Search for your business name. See what comes up first: your Facebook page, a directory listing, a competitor, or nothing.
  3. Open your Facebook page in a private browser window, logged out, and see what a stranger sees.
  4. Check who can get into your page. Make sure the business can still get in if any of them leaves or loses their phone.
  5. If you have a Business Profile, look at its website field. If it's empty, a customer who finds you on the map has no website to visit to learn more.

If step 1 shows other businesses and not you, and you want that work, that's your answer.
