What Is a Landing Page? Why It Matters for Lead Generation

A landing page is a web page with one job: turning visitors into leads. Here is what makes one convert well, and why you need one for paid ads.

What is a landing page?

A landing page is a dedicated web page built for one specific marketing goal, with a single call to action and no navigation links. It's the destination you send traffic from Google Ads and Meta Ads campaigns, email campaigns, or SEO visitors to when you want them to take a specific action, typically booking a call, requesting a quote, or submitting their contact details.

The word "landing" is literal: it is the page someone lands on right after clicking something specific. Because you know what they clicked, you can build the page around that one thing and delete the rest. That is why a page built around one ad can turn more of its visitors into enquiries than a homepage does. Yuppiechef, the South African online kitchen store, took the menu off its wedding registry landing page, and sign-ups rose from 3% to 6% of visitors. The test was published by VWO, which sells testing software.

Four things separate a landing page from a homepage:

  • One conversion goal. Book a call or request a quote, not three actions competing.
  • No global navigation. Every nav link is an exit you paid for.
  • Copy written for one segment. One offer, one problem, one service area.
  • Its own tracking. A dedicated URL and thank-you page, so conversion rate is measurable per campaign.

Landing page vs homepage: what actually changes

Most people asking this already have a website, so the real question is "why can't I just use my homepage?" Because your homepage has a different job: introduce the company, serve customers hunting for a phone number, route job applicants, and hand visitors to deeper pages. A typical small business homepage carries 25 to 40 clickable links. Unbounce's Oli Gardner named this the attention ratio: things a visitor can do, divided by things you want them to do. A homepage runs 40:1. A landing page should run 1:1.

The homepage is still right for brand searches and referrals. It is wrong for a $9 click from someone who typed "emergency ac repair near me" forty seconds ago.

Landing page conversion rate benchmarks

In an analysis of Google Ads accounts, WordStream, which sells online advertising software, puts the average landing page conversion rate at 2.35%, the top 25% at 5.31% and the top 10% above 11%. Unbounce, which sells a landing page builder, puts the median across industries at 6.6% in its 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report. Neither is your number: industry and traffic source drive conversion rate more than design does.

What a conversion rate improvement is worth

A home services business spending $8,000 a month at a $9 cost per click buys roughly 890 clicks. Change only the destination:

  • Homepage at 1.8%: 16 leads, $500 per lead
  • Basic landing page at 4%: 36 leads, $222 per lead
  • Optimised landing page at 8%: 71 leads, $113 per lead

At a 25% close rate on a $6,000 average job, that is roughly $24,000 versus $106,500 in booked revenue from identical spend. Fix the page before raising the budget. To get an idea of what clicks cost for the searches you want to show up for, look in Google Ads' Keyword Planner: it shows a top-of-page bid range for each keyword, from what advertisers have historically paid.

The anatomy of a high-converting landing page

Every element should have a job. If you can't name it in one sentence, delete the element.

1. Headline

Job: confirm in three seconds that the visitor is in the right place. It restates the promise of the ad that delivered them. If a stranger could not pair ad headline with page headline, fix that first.

2. Subheadline

Job: answer "what is this, and is it for me?" One or two sentences naming the audience, outcome, and timeframe. "We help roofing companies in Denver generate 20+ qualified leads per month" beats "We help businesses grow."

3. Hero visual

Job: prove you are real before anyone reads a word. A photo of your actual crew, van, or finished work beats stock, which reads as generic. It is also the usual speed failure: keep it under 200KB.

4. The offer

Job: give a reason to act now instead of later. "Contact us" is not an offer. "Free 20-minute roof inspection with a photo report, booked within 48 hours" is. The bigger the ticket, the smaller the first ask.

5. Social proof

Job: let someone other than you do the persuading. BrightLocal (2024) found 87% of consumers read online reviews before contacting a local business. One named review with a specific detail beats five anonymous five-star quotes. No reviews yet? Use licences, certifications, or a response-time commitment, and never make up reviews.

6. Lead capture form

Job: capture the minimum you need for a useful conversation. Every field costs conversion, and drop-off steepens past about four. Name, phone, and email is enough for most service businesses; budget range is the field people abandon on.

7. Call to action

Job: make the next step unambiguous. Button copy should finish "I want to...": "Get My Free Quote" outperforms "Submit". One CTA type per page. On mobile, a sticky call button often converts best.

8. Trust signals

Job: remove the last objection. Licence and insurance numbers, a service-area map, a real address, a visible phone number, a privacy line under the form. Nobody reads them closely; everybody notices their absence.

9. Speed and mobile

Job: stop losing people before the page loads. Google's "good" Largest Contentful Paint threshold is 2.5 seconds on mobile, and over 60% of Google Ads clicks happen on phones.

Landing pages for paid traffic

With organic traffic, a weak page costs you a visitor. With paid traffic, it costs you the visitor and the money you spent buying them.

What matters most is continuity: query, then ad copy, then page headline, then form. Break the chain anywhere and people bounce. If your Google Ads campaign promises "Emergency AC Repair, 2-Hour Response", the page should open with those words, not "Welcome to Smith Heating & Air, Serving Dallas Since 1998." Three things follow:

  • One page per offer, not per campaign. "AC repair" and "AC installation" need separate pages: one buyer has a deadline, the other is comparing prices.
  • Cold traffic needs a longer page. A high-intent searcher is ready for a form above the fold. Someone interrupted mid-scroll on Facebook needs proof first.
  • The page changes what you pay per click. Google scores landing page experience as one of three Quality Score components, so a better page can lower your CPC.

Tracking is the other argument. A landing page gives you a clean thank-you URL to fire conversions on; without it, Google's automatic bidding learns from muddled data. And on a homepage, the nav bar is an exit ramp you rented.

7 landing page mistakes that quietly kill conversion rate

  1. Sending paid traffic to the homepage. The most expensive mistake here, and the most common.
  2. One page behind six ad groups. Guarantees a message match failure in at least five of them.
  3. A headline about you. "Welcome to our website" says nothing about whether their problem gets solved.
  4. Asking too much, too early. Nine fields, a budget dropdown, and a "tell us about your project" box.
  5. No conversion tracking. If you can't see which keyword produced a lead, you'll cut the campaign that works.
  6. A hero image that tanks load speed. A 3MB header photo loses mobile visitors before they see anything.
  7. Leaving the navigation in. Usually because the page was built inside the main site template.

Pre-launch landing page checklist

Run this before a single ad goes live. It takes twenty minutes and catches the failures that cost the most.

  • Message match. The headline echoes the ad closely enough to pair them.
  • One goal. Count every link; anything that is not the CTA gets justified or removed.
  • Form tested end to end. Submit a real test lead; confirm it reaches the inbox and CRM.
  • Autoresponder fires within 60 seconds, and not into spam.
  • Conversion tracking verified firing, not merely installed: Google Ads, Meta Pixel and CAPI, GA4.
  • Thank-you page on its own URL. You cannot fire a conversion reliably on a static URL.
  • Real-device mobile check. Resizing a desktop browser hides tap-target and keyboard problems.
  • Tappable phone number and a sticky call button on mobile.
  • PageSpeed Insights, mobile tab. Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds.
  • Spam protection. A honeypot field or reCAPTCHA, or week one is all bots.
  • Privacy policy link. Required by Google Ads and Meta lead policies.
  • A response plan. Someone calls new leads within five minutes. No page outruns a two-day callback.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a landing page if I already have a website?

Yes. Sending paid ad traffic to your website homepage is one of the most common and costly mistakes in digital marketing. Homepages have too many distractions, too many options, and no single focus. A landing page for each offer you advertise takes those distractions away. In one test published by VWO, which sells testing software, taking the menu off a landing page doubled its sign-ups, from 3% to 6% of visitors.

How much does a landing page cost?

It depends on who builds it and what the price includes, so ask for a list of what you get. Landing pages and other custom builds are not in our website packages; we price them on your brief.

What is a good landing page conversion rate?

WordStream, which sells online advertising software, puts the average landing page conversion rate at 2.35%, the top 25% at 5.31% or better and the top 10% above 11%. Unbounce, which sells a landing page builder, puts the median across industries at 6.6% in its 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report. Judge yourself against your own traffic source and past months, not a global average.

How long should a landing page be?

Match the length to the traffic. High-intent search visitors convert best on short pages of roughly 400 to 800 words with the form above the fold. Cold traffic from Meta or display needs 1,000 to 1,500 words of context, proof, and objection handling first. Answer every question standing between the visitor and the form, then stop.

Should I send Google Ads traffic to my homepage or a landing page?

A landing page, almost always. Your homepage serves prospects, customers, and job seekers at once, so it carries a full menu and a dozen exit links you are paying for. A landing page removes those exits and answers the query the ad was bought for. It also gives you a clean thank-you URL for conversion tracking, and landing page experience feeds Quality Score, so a better page lowers your cost per click.
