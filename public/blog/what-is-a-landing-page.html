What Is a Landing Page? Why It Matters for Lead Generation

A landing page is a web page with one job: turning visitors into leads. Here is what makes one convert well, and why you need one for paid ads.

What is a landing page?

A landing page is a dedicated web page built for one specific marketing goal, with a single call to action and no navigation links. It's the page you send people to from Google Ads and Meta Ads campaigns, email campaigns or unpaid search results when you want them to take a specific action, such as booking a call, requesting a quote or submitting their contact details.

The word "landing" is literal: it is the page someone lands on right after clicking something specific. Because you know what they clicked, you can build the page around that one thing and delete the rest. That is why a page built around one ad can turn more of its visitors into enquiries than a homepage does. Yuppiechef, the South African online kitchen store, took the menu off its wedding registry landing page, and sign-ups rose from 3% to 6% of visitors. The test was published by VWO, which sells testing software.

Four things separate a landing page from a homepage:

  • One conversion goal. Book a call or request a quote, not three actions competing.
  • No global navigation. Every nav link is an exit you paid for.
  • Copy written for one segment. One offer, one problem, one service area.
  • Its own tracking. A dedicated URL and thank-you page, so conversion rate is measurable per campaign.

Landing page vs homepage: what actually changes

If you already have a website, the real question is "why can't I just use my homepage?" Because your homepage has a different job: introduce the company, serve customers hunting for a phone number, route job applicants, and hand visitors to deeper pages. Count the links on your own homepage: the menu, the footer and every button to another page. Each one is something a visitor can do instead of the one thing you want them to do. A landing page cuts that down to one.

The homepage is still right for brand searches and referrals. It is the wrong place for a paid click from someone who typed "emergency plumber centurion" forty seconds ago.

Landing page conversion rate benchmarks

Conversion rate is the share of visitors who do the one thing the page asks. In a 2014 analysis of Google Ads accounts, WordStream, which sells online advertising software, put the average landing page conversion rate at 2.35%, the top 25% at 5.31% and the top 10% above 11%. Unbounce, which sells a landing page builder, puts the median across industries at 6.6% in its 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report. Neither is your number. The one to beat is your own page's rate, measured month by month and by where the clicks came from.

Fix the page before you raise the budget

On the same ad budget, a page that turns twice as many visitors into enquiries halves what each enquiry costs you. To run the sums for your own trade, start with what a click costs: Google Ads' Keyword Planner shows a top-of-page bid range for each keyword, from what advertisers have historically paid.

The anatomy of a high-converting landing page

Every element should have a job. If you can't name it in one sentence, delete the element.

1. Headline

Job: confirm at a glance that the visitor is in the right place. It restates the promise of the ad that delivered them. If a stranger couldn't match your ad's headline to your page's headline, fix that first.

2. Subheadline

Job: answer "what is this, and is it for me?" One or two sentences naming the audience, outcome, and timeframe. "Burst geyser in Centurion? A plumber at your door today" beats "We help businesses grow."

3. Hero visual

Job: prove you are real before anyone reads a word. A photo of your actual crew, van, or finished work beats stock, which reads as generic. A heavy photo also slows the page: keep it under 200KB.

4. The offer

Job: give a reason to act now instead of later. "Free 20-minute roof inspection with a photo report, booked within 48 hours" is an offer. "Contact us" is just a button label. The bigger the ticket, the smaller the first ask.

5. Social proof

Job: let someone other than you do the persuading. One named review with a specific detail beats five anonymous five-star quotes. No reviews yet? Use licences, certifications, or a response-time commitment, and never make up reviews.

6. Lead capture form

Job: capture the minimum you need for a useful conversation. Every extra field is one more reason to stop filling it in. Name, phone and email is enough to start a conversation; ask the rest on the call.

7. Call to action

Job: make the next step unambiguous. Button copy should finish "I want to...": "Get My Free Quote", for example, rather than "Submit". One CTA type per page. On phones, a call button that stays on screen keeps the next step in reach.

8. Trust signals

Job: remove the last objection. Licence and insurance numbers, a service-area map, a real address, a visible phone number, a privacy line under the form. Nobody reads them closely; everybody notices their absence.

9. Speed and mobile

Job: stop losing people before the page loads. Google counts a Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less as good. That is the time the biggest image or block of text on screen takes to appear. And in South Africa, 77.39% of web page views came from mobile phones in October 2026, by Statcounter's count.

Landing pages for paid traffic

With organic traffic, a weak page costs you a visitor. With paid traffic, it costs you the visitor and the money you spent buying them.

What matters most is message match: query, then ad copy, then page headline, then form. Break the chain anywhere and people bounce. If your Google Ads campaign promises "Burst Geyser? Plumber There in 2 Hours", the page should open with those words, not "Welcome to Smith Plumbing, Serving Pretoria Since 1998." Three more things matter when you pay for clicks:

  • One page per offer, not per campaign. "Geyser repair" and "geyser installation" need separate pages: one buyer has a deadline, the other is comparing prices.
  • Cold traffic needs a longer page. Someone who searched for exactly your service is ready for a form at the top of the page. Someone interrupted mid-scroll on Facebook needs proof first.
  • Google rates the page. Landing page experience is one of the three parts of Quality Score, Google's check on how your ads and pages compare with other advertisers'.

Tracking is the other reason to give your ads their own page. A landing page gives you a clean thank-you URL to fire conversions on; without it, Google's automatic bidding learns from muddled data.

7 landing page mistakes that quietly kill conversion rate

  1. Sending paid traffic to the homepage. You pay for the click, then hand the visitor a page full of exits.
  2. One page behind six ad groups. At most one of the six can match its ad.
  3. A headline about you. "Welcome to our website" says nothing about whether their problem gets solved.
  4. Asking too much, too early. Nine fields, a budget dropdown, and a "tell us about your project" box.
  5. No conversion tracking. If you can't see which keyword produced a lead, you'll cut the campaign that works.
  6. A hero image that tanks load speed. A 3MB header photo loses mobile visitors before they see anything.
  7. Leaving the navigation in. It creeps in when the page is built inside the main site template.

Pre-launch landing page checklist

Run this before a single ad goes live. It is quick, and it catches the failures that waste ad money.

  • Message match. The headline echoes the ad closely enough to pair them.
  • One goal. Count every link; anything that is not the CTA gets justified or removed.
  • Form tested end to end. Submit a real test lead; confirm it reaches the inbox and CRM.
  • Autoresponder fires within 60 seconds, and not into spam.
  • Conversion tracking verified firing, not merely installed: Google Ads, Meta Pixel and CAPI, GA4.
  • Thank-you page on its own URL. If the address stays the same after the form is sent, you cannot count the conversion by URL.
  • Real-device mobile check. Resizing a desktop browser hides tap-target and keyboard problems.
  • Tappable phone number and a sticky call button on mobile.
  • PageSpeed Insights, mobile tab. Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less.
  • Spam protection. A honeypot field or reCAPTCHA, or bots fill your inbox.
  • Privacy policy link. Required by Google Ads and Meta lead policies.
  • A response plan. Someone calls new leads within five minutes. No page outruns a two-day callback.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a landing page if I already have a website?

Yes. Sending paid ad traffic to your homepage is a costly mistake. A homepage has too many ways out and no single focus. A landing page for each offer you advertise closes them off. In one test published by VWO, which sells testing software, taking the menu off a landing page doubled its sign-ups, from 3% to 6% of visitors.

How much does a landing page cost?

It depends on who builds it and what the price includes, so ask for a list of what you get. Landing pages and other custom builds are not in our website packages; we price them on your brief.

What is a good landing page conversion rate?

In 2014, WordStream, which sells online advertising software, put the average landing page conversion rate at 2.35%, the top 25% at 5.31% or better and the top 10% above 11%. Unbounce, which sells a landing page builder, puts the median across industries at 6.6% in its 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report. Judge yourself against your own traffic source and past months, not a global average.

How long should a landing page be?

Match the length to the traffic. People who searched for exactly your service are ready for a short page with the form near the top. Cold traffic from Meta or display needs more context, proof and objection handling first. Answer every question standing between the visitor and the form, then stop.

Should I send Google Ads traffic to my homepage or a landing page?

A landing page, almost always. Your homepage serves prospects, customers, and job seekers at once, so it carries a full menu and other exit links you are paying for. A landing page removes those exits and answers the query the ad was bought for. It also gives you a clean thank-you URL for conversion tracking, and Google rates landing page experience as part of its Quality Score.
