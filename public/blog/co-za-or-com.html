Should your business use .co.za or .com?

If your customers are in South Africa, .co.za is the natural choice: it tells people, and Google, that you're a South African business. A .com works well too, and makes sense if you sell outside the country or the .co.za you want is taken. Either way, the ending matters less than who the domain is registered to and whether it renews on time.

What .co.za tells your customers and Google

.ZA is South Africa's country code, administered by ZADNA, the .ZA Domain Name Authority. You can't register a name straight under .za: it goes under a second level such as co.za, which is the ending businesses use.

Google explains that country-code domains are tied to a country and give a strong signal to both users and search engines that a site is meant for that country. For a plumber in Centurion, a builder in Polokwane or a salon in Durbanville, that's exactly the message you want. A customer who sees yourbusiness.co.za on your bakkie or your invoice knows straight away you're local.

A .com can still be found in South Africa

Google treats .com as a generic ending, so it looks at other things to work out who a .com site is for. On the same page, Google lists local addresses and phone numbers on the pages, local language and currency, links from other local sites, and signals from your Business Profile.

So a .com with a Centurion number, prices in rand and links from South African sites still reads as South African. We've built sites on both endings: The Vault SA runs on .co.za, and Tiny Homes SA runs on .com. You can see both on our work page.

Where .com earns its place is a business that sells, or plans to sell, beyond South Africa, or a brand name where the .co.za is gone.

Don't confuse .co with .co.za

This catches people out. .co is a different ending altogether, and Google treats .co as generic, along with endings like .io and .tv. It says nothing about South Africa. If a customer drops the .za when typing your address, they end up at a different domain, which may belong to someone else.

How to choose

  1. If your customers are in South Africa and you work locally, pick .co.za.
  2. If you sell across borders, or want a name that travels, .com is a sound choice. Put your South African phone number, the areas you serve and rand prices on the site so Google can still place you.
  3. If both are available and you want to protect the name, register both, choose the main address, and send the other to it. Google recommends a permanent server-side redirect for exactly that kind of move, so people and Google end up in the same place.
  4. Say it out loud before you buy it. You'll read your address over the phone and print it on the van. If you keep having to spell it, choose something plainer.

Remember the domain also becomes the end of your email address. An address like info@yourbusiness.co.za on a quote tells your customer who it's from before they open it.

Register it in your business's name

This part matters more than the ending. Names under co.za are first come, first served, and the registry's published policy says the registrar supplies the registrant's full legal name, email, phone number and physical address. The registrant is whoever those details describe. As long as the domain is kept current, the registrant can keep using it.

So check that the registrant is you or your business, and not your web designer, your nephew or the agency you used a while back. Make sure the email on the record is an inbox you actually read.

Renewal is the other trap. Under the same policy, co.za names renew automatically by default, but if the status is set to "non-renew", cancellation starts on the renewal date. After a name has been deleted it can't be reinstated, only registered again as new, and on a first come, first served register that might not be by you.

The policy also says registrants must be able to choose the registrar they use, so you can move the name away from whoever first registered it for you.

If somebody else has registered a name that copies your business's name, ZADNA publishes Alternative Dispute Resolution Regulations for abusive or offensive registrations. That's general information, so speak to an attorney about your own situation.
