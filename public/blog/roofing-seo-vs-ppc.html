Roofing SEO vs PPC: which one wins storm work, and which wins retail jobs?

PPC can be aimed at the zip codes a hail storm hit. SEO wins the retail roofs between storms. What both need from you, and which to start with.

Roofing SEO vs PPC comes down to the two kinds of work you sell. PPC, meaning Google search ads, can be switched on after a hail storm and aimed at the zip codes it hit. SEO keeps you visible for the retail replacements and repairs people search for between storms, rankings that Google says no ad spend can buy. If you want both kinds of work, run both, with call and form tracking set up first.

Storm work and retail work reach a roofer through search in different ways. One arrives as a rush of calls from the streets a storm crossed. The other comes from homeowners whose roof is old or leaking, and no storm decides when they call.

This post takes the two in turn, then covers what both channels need from you and where to start.

After a hail storm, does SEO or PPC get the phone ringing?

PPC does, because you can aim it at the part of town the storm crossed, where the homeowners who need you live. Google Ads location targeting goes down to zip codes or a radius around a point. Check the location option as well: by default Google also shows ads to people who have only shown interest in those places, and the Presence option keeps them to people in or regularly in the area. The budget can go where the damage is instead of across your whole service area.

Organic results cannot be aimed like that. You cannot buy an organic ranking, and Google warns that no one can guarantee a top spot. Whatever position you hold when the hail falls is the one you work with. The map has a limit too. Google's help on local ranking names distance from the person searching as one of the main factors. A storm on the far side of the metro can land outside the area where your listing tends to show.

Say you run a roofing company in Plano and a May hail storm crosses a few neighbourhoods on the east side of town. If a storm campaign is already built and paused, you switch it on and point it at the zip codes in the storm's path. The clicks go to a storm inspection page. Switching campaigns off and on has two traps. A removed campaign cannot be resumed or restored, so pause storm campaigns between storms instead of removing them. And resuming a campaign does not restart ad groups or keywords you paused separately.

Our roofing marketing page explains how we build storm campaigns ahead of time. For North Texas, our page on lead generation in Dallas-Fort Worth sets out how the Metroplex's weather seasons shape demand.

What brings in retail roof jobs between storms?

SEO and your Google Business Profile, with ads on the searches from people ready to book.

Retail work does not wait for weather. It starts when a roof gets old, or when a buyer's inspection flags it before a sale. These homeowners are paying for it themselves and have time to compare. Before they call anyone, they may search whether to repair or replace, or which material suits the house.

A page for each service you offer, plus pages that answer those questions, can bring them in without a charge for each visit. Google says it costs nothing to appear in its organic results. Ads still earn a place on retail searches where someone wants a price now, such as a replacement quote in a named town. On the early questions, an ad buys a click from someone who is still reading.

Nobody can promise you a date for organic rankings. The pace depends on your site's history and local competition, among other things, all covered in how long SEO takes.

Can one page sell insurance work and retail work?

Not well, and the cost shows up in both channels.

The homeowner with hail damage is reacting to the weather. Their questions are about your inspection and what happens next with the insurer. The retail homeowner is making a planned purchase and wants to know what the job includes and what the warranty covers.

In PPC, the split means separate campaigns. Google Ads budgets are set per campaign, so storm spend can rise and fall without touching the retail ads. It also means separate landing pages, because Google's ad auction weighs how well a landing page matches what the ad led people to expect. In SEO, it means a page for each service. The storm damage inspection page stays up all year, so it can rank between storms too.

Keep the insurance page to what you control: how your inspection works and what you hand the homeowner afterwards. The insurer decides the claim, so a page that promises approval promises something you cannot deliver. Questions about coverage belong with the homeowner's insurer.

What should you fix before you blame SEO or PPC?

Check the groundwork. Without it, both channels look worse than they are.

Start with tracking. A call from an ad sounds just like a call from the map, so unless something records the difference, you cannot compare the channels. Google Ads can count a call from an ad as a conversion once it passes a minimum length you set. Forms need a conversion action of their own. Our guide to conversion tracking for lead generation sets it up step by step, including how to tell Google Ads which leads became signed roofs. Ask every caller how they found you and write it down, so yard signs and neighbours' referrals get counted too.

Both channels use area pages, and they are easy to get wrong. Google's spam policies define doorway abuse as pages "created to rank for specific, similar search queries". One example they give is pages "targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page". A Frisco page that is your Plano page with the town name swapped fits that description. The same policies count a block of town names at the foot of a page as keyword stuffing. An area page earns its place by telling a homeowner there something useful, such as how the city handles roofing permits.

You cannot cover extra towns with extra map listings. Google's Business Profile help allows a service-area business one profile for the whole area it serves. If you do not serve customers at your address, it says to remove the address.

Then reviews. More reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking, Google says, and a homeowner who sees your ad may look you up and read them before calling. Ask every customer. Google's rules for Maps forbid offering anything in exchange for a review, and asking only the customers you expect to be happy. They also say a business should not request specific content. So no discount in return for a review, and no asking customers to mention "roof replacement in Plano".

Will PPC help your roofing website rank?

No. Google puts this in writing on its page about hiring an SEO. Buying ads does not change your site's place in the organic results, and in its own words Google "never accepts money to include or rank sites". The map is no different: the Business Profile help says a better local ranking cannot be requested or bought.

Search Console can save you ad spend. Its Performance report lists the queries that send you organic visitors. Check it before you buy ads on a retail search, because you may already show for that search without paying per click.

Ads still give you something SEO can use. Your search terms report lists the searches that triggered your ads, apart from low-volume ones Google holds back for privacy. Google suggests using it to discover new ideas for landing page content. After a storm, it shows the wording of the more common searches your storm ads appeared for, which is worth knowing when you rewrite your storm damage page.

Where should a roofer's first dollar go, SEO or PPC?

Into tracking. After that, our usual starting point is search ads and a page built for them, with local SEO added once the ads are booking inspections. That is the order our roofing marketing page gives. It suits a roofer who needs work now and whose website cannot yet win it in search.

The pages the ads need are pages SEO needs too, so that work carries over. Build the storm campaigns in the same stretch, check them and pause them. When hail comes, the only decisions left are how much to spend and where.

The order can flip. If you already show on the map in your own town and have plenty of reviews, the website may be the weak link. Fix the service and area pages first, and keep the ads for storms and for towns where the map does not show you.

Google's Local Services Ads are a separate decision, if they are offered for roofers where you work. You pay for each valid lead rather than each click, and the business has to pass Google's checks first. Our comparison of Local Services Ads and Google Ads covers the trade-offs, roofing included.

What should you check before the next storm?

Start with your Google Ads account, if you have one. Check whether any storm campaigns from last season are paused or removed, and that each one still sends clicks to a page that matches it.

Then, from a phone that has never called you, open your website and call the number on it. Find that call in your call tracking and job records, with the source it was given. If it is missing, fix that before you spend on either channel.

Next, search on a phone for your main service and a town you work in. Do it from that town if you can, since Google weighs distance from the searcher. See where you appear, if anywhere, and read your reviews as a stranger would.

What you find tells you where the first dollar goes. Missing tracking comes first, whatever else you decide, and missing pages hold back both channels.
