Cost Per Lead Benchmarks by Industry: What's a Good CPL in 2026?

A benchmark is only useful once you know your own ceiling. Published industry figures below, with who measured them, then the calculation that turns them into the most you can afford to pay.

What Is Cost Per Lead (CPL)?

Cost per lead (CPL) is the total amount spent on advertising divided by the number of leads generated. CPL = Total Ad Spend / Number of Leads. It is the everyday control number for paid advertising in a service business. Budgets are better set on cost per closed deal, as this page shows.

Many agency reports lead with clicks, impressions, and click-through rates. Those are vanity metrics. The number that decides whether your marketing is profitable is cost per qualified lead. Below are the latest published benchmarks and who measured them, the maths for what you can afford, and why CPL alone will mislead you. Our lead generation services for service businesses are built around this metric.

What actually counts as a lead

Before comparing your CPL to anyone else's, check you're counting the same thing. A "lead" in one report is a newsletter signup; in another it is a booked in-home estimate.

  • Raw lead. Any form fill, call, or chat, spam and wrong numbers included. Quoted when someone wants the CPL to look good.
  • Qualified lead. Right service, right area, real contact details, genuine intent. The share of raw leads that qualifies varies by channel and by how the form is built, so measure your own.

The published benchmarks below count every conversion an advertiser tracked, which puts them closer to raw leads than qualified ones. Your cost per qualified lead will be higher than the matching figure.

Paid Acquisition CPL vs Organic CPL Over Time

Paid leads arrive as soon as the ads run, and you pay for every click for as long as they run. A long-term SEO strategy trades that per-click charge for an upfront cost, so effective organic CPL (monthly SEO spend divided by organic leads) falls as a site gains rankings, and the rankings do not switch off the day you stop paying, the way ads do. To take a made-up example, a $4,000 a month programme producing 10 leads in month three is a $400 effective CPL; if it produces 70 leads in month twelve, that is $57. Judge the mix on blended cost per closed deal.

What Is a Lead Worth to You?

Every benchmark on this page is a cost. None of them tells you whether that cost is good, because that depends entirely on what a lead is worth to your business. Lead value is the number to work out first.

The formula is short. A lead is worth your average customer value multiplied by your close rate. Apply gross margin and you get profit per lead, which is the figure that governs what you can afford to pay. For example, a $2,500 customer at a 15% close rate and a 40% margin makes each lead worth $150 in gross profit, which supports a $45 cost per lead if you spend 30% of gross profit on acquisition.

That calculation, with worked examples across six industries and a calculator for your own numbers, is in our guide to how much a lead is worth. Run it before you use any benchmark on this page to judge a quote, because the same $80 lead is a bargain in one business and a slow loss in another.

How to Calculate Your Maximum Acceptable CPL

Benchmarks tell you what other people pay. Only your unit economics tell you what you can afford.

Max CPL = (Average Deal Value x Gross Margin) x Lead-to-Close Rate x Share of Gross Profit Spent on Acquisition
The first two terms give gross profit per customer. Multiply by close rate for gross profit per lead, then by your acquisition share for the ceiling you can pay and still make money.

Use gross margin, not revenue: a $10,000 job at 30% margin is a $3,000 job as far as marketing is concerned. If that ceiling comes out below what leads actually cost in your industry, the honest conclusion is that the channel does not clear yet. We walk through that decision in full in is lead generation worth it.

The contractor's hard wall is $693, the full gross profit per lead. At $173 they keep $520 per lead: spend $6,000 a month at a $150 CPL, buy 40 qualified leads, close about nine jobs, and generate roughly $28,350 in gross profit against that $6,000 of ad spend.

The SaaS ceiling is fragile in a different way. A $4,800 CAC against $400 of monthly gross profit is exactly a 12-month payback. Cut retention to 18 months and lifetime gross profit falls to $7,200, the ceiling drops to $2,400, and max CPL halves to $288. Retention sets this ceiling, and bidding only changes what you actually pay against it. Recurring revenue at an 80% margin is why, in this example, SaaS can pay more than three times what the contractor can.

Why CPL Alone Is the Wrong Metric

CPL is trivially easy to move the wrong way. Loosen targeting, cut the form to a name and email, offer a giveaway, and CPL can drop fast while revenue does not move. Here is a worked example with made-up figures: two campaigns for the same HVAC replacement company, each spending $6,000, with the $3,150 gross profit per job from the example above. Either way, measure the full ladder: cost per raw lead, cost per qualified lead, cost per booked appointment, cost per closed deal, and CAC as a share of gross profit. That last one is the scoreboard. It needs call tracking, a CRM stage on every lead, and offline conversion imports into Google Ads, or you are simply telling Google to find the cheapest lead.

Why Your CPL Is Higher Than Benchmarks (And How to Fix It)

1. Poor Landing Page Conversion Rate

A big lever is the page the click lands on. A homepage has to serve every visitor at once. A dedicated landing page built for one offer, with copy that matches the ad, one call to action, and a short form, gives a paid click one obvious next step. Conversion rate feeds straight into CPL: at the same cost per click, lifting conversion rate from 2% to 8% cuts cost per lead to a quarter without touching a bid. Position Xero builds conversion-focused landing pages for exactly this. Not sure where yours leaks? Start with a free SEO audit.

2. Broad Keyword Targeting

Broad match can show your ad on searches that have little to do with what you sell. Check the search terms report, tighten to phrase or exact match where broad match drifts, and add negative keywords, which Google describes as a way to focus on only the keywords that matter to your customers.

3. Low Quality Score

Google says higher quality ads typically cost less per click and get better ad positions. Quality Score, the 1 to 10 rating in your account, is the diagnostic Google gives you for that. Google is clear that the score itself is not an input in the ad auction, so use it to find the ad, keyword or landing page that needs work. That work is the core of our Google Ads management services.

4. Your Market and Your Season

A national median does not tell you what your market costs. Google's own guide to what Google Ads costs says competition influences price, and that seasonal trends and targeting choices such as location play a significant role in final costs. The same keyword can cost more in a city where more businesses bid on it, and in seasonal trades, such as HVAC in a heat wave or roofing after a hail storm, costs can rise when demand and competing bids jump together. Compare the same month year over year.

5. Your Tracking Is Lying to You

Many CPL problems are measurement problems: one person counted twice for filling a form and then calling; newsletter signups and job applications counted as leads; conversions counted per click instead of per unique lead; no offline import, so a $40,000 job looks identical to a bot submission. Fix attribution before you touch bids.
