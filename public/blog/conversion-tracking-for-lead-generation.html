Conversion Tracking for Lead Generation: Track Booked Jobs, Not Form Fills

Your account probably counts form fills and phone taps. Here is how to make it count booked jobs instead, in what order, and how to check the number against your books.

Conversion tracking for lead generation, in short

Most service businesses have conversion tracking installed and still cannot say which ads produced customers, because the thing being counted is a form submission or a phone tap rather than a booked job. Fixing it means tracking the lead through every stage, feeding the outcome back to Google, and reconciling the report against your invoices once a month.

Say a contractor opens Google Ads and sees 62 conversions against $4,300 of spend. That is $69 a conversion, which reads like a good month. Then the bookkeeper finds eight new jobs from the website. Both numbers are accurate. Only one describes a customer.

The tag in that account fires on the wrong event. Google counts the moment somebody touched a form; the business counts the moment somebody signed. Nobody reconciles the two, so the campaign producing the most phone taps keeps its budget and the one producing jobs gets cut. The point of lead generation is a booked job, so that is what tracking has to count.

Google Ads sees a click and a tag firing afterwards, tied together by a click identifier that its auto-tagging documentation calls the GCLID, short for "Google Click Identifier". Everything past that tag happens outside the browser. Google does not know whether the call was answered, whether the caller is in your service area, or whether the invoice was paid, unless you send it.

The four things most accounts count instead of leads

Look first at what the account already counts. Four problems come up repeatedly, and all four inflate the number.

  • Thank-you page loads, not form submissions. If the conversion fires on a view of /thank-you, anything that loads that URL fires it: a refresh, a back-button hit, the confirmation link in your reply email. Google's conversion counting documentation confirms that "Every conversion" is the default counting setting for website conversion actions.
  • Every tel: tap treated as a lead. A tap is an intention to dial. It counts the misdial, the hang-up at the hold music, the existing customer chasing an invoice. Google's own call method sets a higher bar: the phone snippet "tracks a call as a conversion when it lasts longer than a minimum length you set".
  • The same person counted twice. Somebody taps the number, does not get through, then comes back and fills in the form. Two conversion actions fire and one human is in your pipeline.
  • Everything marked primary. Google's page on primary and secondary conversion actions says primary ones "are reported in the 'Conversions' column in your reports and used for bidding as long as the standard goal they are part of is used for bidding". Secondary ones "are for observation only", reported in All conversions "but not for bidding", unless they sit inside a custom goal. Google's Target CPA documentation adds that "Smart Bidding will learn across all conversion actions reported in the 'Conversions' column".

Whatever sits in that column is what the algorithm goes shopping for. Put a newsletter signup in there and you are paying Google to find people who sign up for newsletters.

Booked jobs are the conversion

A lead moves through stages: click, enquiry, contacted, qualified, quoted, booked, paid. Google Ads sees the first two. Your money is at booked and paid, and everything between is invisible until somebody tells it.

Underneath the counting problem sits a value problem. A blocked drain and a full system replacement are not the same event, and if both report a bare "1" to Smart Bidding, the algorithm has no reason to prefer the one worth ten times more. Conversion values fix that, and we do the arithmetic in how much a lead is actually worth.

Why Google Ads and Analytics will never match

Google publishes a page on data discrepancies listing ten factors. Four matter most here.

  • The date is different. "Google Ads reports conversions on the ad impression date. Other reporting tools attribute them to the conversion date." A September click that books in October sits in a different month in each tool.
  • The number is not final. "Conversions can be reported up to 90 days after the click." Last month's cost per conversion is a draft.
  • Several windows run at once. Google's conversion window documentation puts the click-through window at 30 days by default, adjustable out to 90, with a one-day view-through and a three-day engaged-view window alongside it.
  • Some of it is modelled, some gets taken back. Cross-device conversions are modelled "to account for users who start their journey on one device with an ad interaction, and complete the conversion on another", and some invalid conversions are "removed if Google detects they may be from spam or invalid traffic, invalid clicks, or have been invalid for longer than 13 months", after the fact.

Google now supports two attribution models, last click and data-driven, with data-driven "the default attribution model for most conversion actions". Analytics renamed its conversions to key events, and Google states that "Google Ads conversions don't appear in standard Google Analytics reports". Use the Conversions column for spending decisions, because bidding reads it, then reconcile against your invoices.

Tracking calls without breaking your listings

For trades, most leads arrive as calls. Google's method swaps the number on the page: the phone snippet "replaces a phone number on your website with a Google forwarding number", and a call counts once it runs longer than a minimum length you choose. Set that threshold just below your shortest call last month that turned into a job.

Google's documentation on those numbers says they "are property of Google, they can change or be reassigned", that you cannot use one "outside of the call reporting", and that after a first call the number keeps connecting to the business for 60 days.

That reassignment clause is why the swapped number should not travel. Google's Business Profile guidelines say to "Provide a phone number that connects to your individual business location" and not to provide numbers that "redirect or 'refer' users to ... phone numbers other than those of the actual business". They never mention call tracking, so treat this as risk management, not a rule: real number on the profile, in citations and in schema, swapped number only for ad visitors.

Recording turns a call into a lead you can grade. Some US states require every party to consent: California Penal Code section 632 penalises a person who "intentionally and without the consent of all parties to a confidential communication, uses an electronic amplifying or recording device to eavesdrop upon or record the confidential communication". Announce it, and check your own states with your own adviser.

South African advertisers hit a specific version of this. Google warns that the Google Tag Manager "standard website call conversion tag is not compatible with international number formats", and says to use the national format without the plus sign.

Feed the back end back in

Everything above stops at the enquiry. The first half of closing the loop is a hidden field: read the GCLID off the landing page URL, write it into a hidden input on every form, and store it against the lead in your CRM. The browser is not a safe place for it. WebKit capped cookies set through document.cookie to a seven-day expiry back in February 2019, so a 30-day window means less than it sounds on iPhones.

The second half is sending the outcome back once the job is booked. Google's offline conversion import page describes the purpose: an ad that "starts a customer down a path that ultimately leads to a sale in the offline world".

Read the notice at the top of that page first: "Starting June 15, 2026, offline conversions import and enhanced conversions for leads uploads will be migrated to the Data Manager API and blocked in the Google Ads API." Google now also says: "If you have not already adopted offline conversion import, we recommend starting with enhanced conversions for leads instead."

Enhanced conversions for leads works on hashed data. Google's page on enhanced conversions says the feature "uses a secure one-way hashing algorithm called SHA256 on your first party customer data, such as email addresses, before sending to Google", then matches it against signed-in accounts. No credible uplift figure exists for it, Google's own page included. Two deadlines bracket the upload: Google will not import a conversion uploaded more than 90 days after its associated last click, and it says to wait 4–6 hours after creating a conversion action before uploading to it.

Meta's Conversions API does the same job from your server or CRM, and server events there are "processed like events sent using the Meta Pixel". Run both channels and the feed belongs in both, which is the unglamorous half of running paid ads properly.

Consent, and why your numbers should go down

Google puts the obligation on the advertiser. Its customer data policies require you to "disclose to customers that you share their information with third-parties to perform ad measurement services on your behalf" and to "obtain customer consent for such third-party sharing and use where legally required". In the United States that means a patchwork of state laws, and Google's guidance says advertisers "should ensure that use of Google products and services ... meets their compliance requirements".

South Africa has a sharper version. POPIA section 11 sets out six lawful grounds for processing personal information. Section 72 then blocks transfers to a recipient in a foreign country unless, among a short list of conditions, that recipient is bound by a law or agreement giving protection substantially similar to POPIA's, or the data subject consents. Uploading a customer's email address to Google is a cross-border transfer, hashed or not. Ask your own legal adviser.

Consent mode carries the answer to the tags. Google describes it as a way to "communicate your users' cookie or app identifier consent status to Google". Basic mode holds the tags until somebody answers the banner; advanced mode loads them and models the gap.

This part costs money to admit. Doing it properly makes your reported conversion count go down. If it goes up after you add consent controls, you are counting people who did not agree to be counted. A lower true number beats a higher false one.

The monthly sanity check

None of this is worth anything until somebody compares the platform's number to the business's. It takes half an hour a month, and almost every guide leaves it out.

Say Google Ads reports 62 conversions on $4,300 of spend, so $69 a conversion. Now open the CRM for the same month. The figures below are invented to show the shape of the problem, not taken from any account.

  • 6 were thank-you page reloads. Same enquiry, counted twice.
  • 18 were phone taps from people who had already submitted the form.
  • Distinct enquiries: 38. That is $113 each.
  • 9 were outside the service area, 4 wanted a service the business does not sell, 3 were existing customers.
  • Qualified leads: 22, at $195 each. Booked jobs: 8, at $538 each.

$69 and $538 describe the same month and the same spend. Only the second can sit next to an average job value. At a $2,400 average job and a 45% gross margin, each job carries $1,080 of gross profit, so $538 still works, with far less headroom than $69 suggested. Optimising toward $69 means buying phone taps.

The process: export the month's conversions, match them to CRM records by date and GCLID, strip duplicates and unqualified, then divide spend by booked jobs. Run it on the same day every month so the reporting lag distorts each month equally.

Then you can judge it. Cost per lead benchmarks by industry covers what the figure should look like, and is lead generation worth the investment asks whether it justifies the spend. Both assume you can measure it accurately.

The order to build this in

Order matters, because several steps are useless without the ones above them.

  1. Turn on auto-tagging. Nothing downstream exists without the GCLID.
  2. One conversion action per real lead action. A form submission and a phone call, not seven variants.
  3. Set the counting to "One" for lead actions. Google's guidance is that one conversion suits you when "you're not interested in the number of sales, but instead whether or not a certain kind of lead was generated".
  4. Mark only the actions you want bought as primary. Everything else goes secondary, reporting without steering bidding.
  5. Fire form conversions on the submit event, not a page load. Covered from the page's side in what a landing page actually is.
  6. Set a minimum call length and use a forwarding number. Stop counting taps.
  7. Capture the GCLID into the CRM. One hidden field on every form.
  8. Send booked jobs back, with values attached. Reporting becomes a bidding input.
  9. Add consent handling, then re-baseline. The numbers will move.
  10. Reconcile monthly. Half an hour, indefinitely.

Steps one to six are roughly a day of work and produce most of the benefit. Seven and eight are where this becomes a system rather than a tag install, and where most projects stall, because the form and the landing page have to carry a hidden field through to the CRM. A site nobody can edit blocks this more often than the tracking does, which is why the site build and the measurement setup are one conversation.
