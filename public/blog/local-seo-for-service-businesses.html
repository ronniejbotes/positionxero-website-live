Local SEO for Service Businesses: How to Rank in Your City

The 5 pillars of local SEO for service businesses in 2026: Google Business Profile, reviews, citations, location pages, and AI SEO. A practical guide.

What is local SEO?

Local SEO is the process of optimising your business to appear in location-based search results on Google, including the local 3-pack (map results), Google Maps, and organic rankings for queries like "plumber near me" or "roofing company in Denver". It matters most to service businesses that work in a defined area, because it is how organic lead generation reaches the people nearby.

A local results page holds two separate rankings: the map pack, and the organic links below it, scored by different systems. You can hold the top organic spot and still be missing from the map pack above it.

How Google actually ranks local businesses

Google names three factors: relevance, distance, and prominence.

  • Relevance is how well your profile and site match the query. You control it with categories, services, and page content.
  • Distance is how far you sit from the searcher. Mostly outside your control, and the reason rank shifts street by street: run a grid check and the same search can rank you near the top outside your own shop and much lower across town.
  • Prominence is how well known you are, which Google says includes how many sites link to you and how many reviews you have.

Google does not publish how much each signal weighs. Read the table as a to-do order: the cheapest work comes first.

Ranking factorEffort
Google Business Profile signalsLow, mostly one-time
Citations (NAP listings)Low, one-time
Review signalsMedium, permanent

Profile and citation work cost the least, so they come first. Links cost the most and come after everything else you control.

Pillar 1: Google Business Profile optimisation

A fully built profile can rank you in the map pack before your website ranks organically. Order of operations:

Categories

Primary category is the most important field on the profile. Pick the most specific one matching the service you want to sell: "roofing contractor" beats "contractor". Add secondary categories only for services you genuinely perform, since irrelevant ones dilute relevance. Calibrate against the primary category of the three profiles already ranking for your keyword.

Services and service areas

List every service separately, each with a short description written the way you would explain it to a customer. A full list of described services gives Google far more to match than three bare names. Service-area businesses should hide the address and set areas by city or postcode, but stay realistic: listing towns you cannot reach in reasonable time weakens the signal for the ones you can serve.

Products

Products is an easy part of the profile for a service business to overlook, and it is worth doing. Package offers with a photo, a fixed price, a description and a link, for example an annual aircon service at its set price. The tiles render inside the profile and give Google and AI answer engines another readable surface.

Photos

Start with a full set: exterior, interior, team, vehicles, work in progress, and before-and-after pairs, then keep adding new ones every month. A profile with no new photos in years looks dormant to the person choosing who to call.

Posts

Post regularly, keep each post short, and give it one call to action. Posts are a weak ranking lever, but they show anyone checking the profile that the business is active.

Pillar 2: reviews and review velocity

Reviews move pack ranking, raise click-through from the pack, and close the sale before the phone rings. Treat them as a process with a monthly number attached.

What actually counts

Count matters relative to your local competitors, not in absolute terms, and so does recency: a long list of old reviews says less to a customer than a steady stream of new ones.

The velocity maths

Take the profile you want to pass. The gap between its count and yours, spread over the months you give yourself, plus what it adds each month, is how many new reviews you need a month. Ask every customer: the share who leave one is your own response rate, and it tells you how many jobs that takes.

How to ask, and how to respond

Ask at job completion, before invoice friction: in person first, then a text the same day with your direct review link. Never screen for happy customers first: Google's review policy bars selectively asking for positive reviews, asking for particular content in a review, and setting staff a number of reviews to collect. Respond to every review promptly, mention the service and the town once on the positive ones, and keep replies to negative ones short. Do not buy reviews: in the US, the FTC's rule of August 2024 bans buying or selling fake reviews and lets the FTC seek civil penalties.

Pillar 3: citations and NAP consistency

A citation is any mention of your business name, address, and phone number on another site: a directory listing with defined fields, or a mention inside page copy like a league sponsorship. They work as verification, since Google cross-references your details to decide how confident it is that you exist where you say you do. A short list of accurate listings beats a long list of bought ones.

The core set worth building by hand

Apple Business Connect, Bing Places, Yelp, Facebook, Nextdoor, BBB, Angi, Thumbtack, Yellow Pages, Foursquare, your Chamber of Commerce, and a few industry directories such as Houzz for remodellers, Avvo for law firms, or Healthgrades for medical practices. Then push the aggregators, Data Axle and Localeze, which feed many smaller sites.

What inconsistency costs you

Google matches entities, not strings, so "Suite 200" versus "Ste. 200" is harmless. Conflicting data is not. The classic: you moved, directories still carry the old address, and Google sees two competing locations and trusts both less. Second: call-tracking numbers scattered across directories, so several phone numbers claim to be you. Fix duplicate Google profiles first, since a duplicate splits reviews and can suppress both listings, then old addresses, phone mismatches, then name variants.

Pillar 4: location pages that are not doorway pages

The standard failure is a set of pages with the same text and the city swapped. Google's spam policies name doorway abuse explicitly, meaning pages made to rank for specific, similar searches, and say sites that break the policies may rank lower or not appear in results at all.

The test before building one: what would someone in this city need to know that someone in the next city would not? If you can't answer, don't build it. What each page genuinely needs:

  • The named crew, tech, or branch covering that area, with a real photo
  • Photos from jobs actually completed in that city
  • Review text from customers in that city, pulled from real reviews
  • Conditions specific to the area: hard water, clay soil movement, hail season, salt corrosion
  • Permit reality, including which office issues it and typical turnaround
  • Coverage detail: the suburbs you cover from that base, and how quickly you usually arrive
  • Pricing that reflects that market, if your pricing genuinely varies
  • Neighbourhoods and landmarks inside real sentences, never as a footer keyword dump
  • A unique title, meta description, H1, and LocalBusiness or Service schema
  • An internal link from a real navigation path, not an orphan only the sitemap knows

A few strong location pages beat many thin ones. Start with the cities where you already have job density and reviews, then add one at a time. Pillar 5: AI SEO for local queries

Google's AI Overviews, and assistants such as ChatGPT and Perplexity, now answer some local questions before anyone opens Maps. Those answers can draw on sources like these:

  1. Your Google Business Profile. Hours, services, ratings, and category are read straight off it, so profile completeness is AI SEO whether you call it that or not.
  2. Your reviews. Models summarise sentiment, so your customers' words become the description of your business. If your reviews keep saying "showed up same day", that phrase can end up in the answer.
  3. Third-party lists. "Best plumbers in your town" roundups are among the pages these answers cite, so you can enter an AI answer through someone else's page first.
  4. Your own pages, when a clean, extractable answer sits right under a heading.

Practically: FAQPage and LocalBusiness schema with an accurate areaServed, one question per heading phrased the way people ask it, a short and direct answer underneath, and published price ranges, which give a model something concrete to quote. The local SEO checklist

In order. Do not skip ahead to links.

  1. Claim and verify your profile, and remove duplicate listings.
  2. Set the most specific primary category, then add only relevant secondaries.
  3. Describe every service individually and set realistic service areas.
  4. Upload real photos and write the full business description.
  5. Add packaged offers as products, with prices and links.
  6. Build the core citations by hand and push the aggregators.
  7. Fix NAP conflicts: duplicates, old addresses, phone mismatches, name variants.
  8. Launch the review request sequence with a monthly target.
  9. Add LocalBusiness and FAQPage schema sitewide.
  10. Rewrite your main service pages with direct answers and price ranges.
  11. Build location pages one at a time, starting where you already have jobs and reviews, each with genuinely local proof.
  12. Post regularly and add new photos every month.
  13. Earn local links: chamber, suppliers, sponsorships, local press.
  14. Run a grid rank check monthly and answer every review promptly, permanently.
