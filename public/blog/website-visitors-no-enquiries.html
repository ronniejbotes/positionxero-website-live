Why does my website get visitors but no enquiries?

Start with the enquiry path. Somewhere between a stranger landing on your site and your phone ringing, something is going wrong: the wrong people are arriving, the right people can't reach you easily from a phone, or enquiries are coming in and nobody is counting them. You can check all of it yourself, with your own phone and Google's own tools, before you pay anyone to redesign anything.

Make sure enquiries really aren't arriving

This sounds obvious. It's also an easy check to skip.

Pick up your phone, switch off Wi-Fi, and open your website the way a customer would. Then:

  1. Fill in your contact form with your own details and send it. Did the email arrive? Look in the spam folder too, and in whichever inbox the form was set up to send to, which may belong to someone who no longer works for you.
  2. Tap your phone number. Does the dialler open with the number filled in? A number baked into a picture can't be tapped, and a number typed as plain text only works if the phone happens to recognise it. A link can use a tel: address, which is what lets a tap start a call.
  3. Tap the WhatsApp button, if your site has it. Does it open a chat with your business number, or with a number nobody watches?
  4. Ask whoever answers the phone to note how each caller found you.

If any of those fail, you may have had enquiries all along that never reached you. And if none of them is being counted, your site can only tell you about visits. Google Analytics lets you send your own events, such as a form sent, a phone number tapped or a WhatsApp chat opened, and mark them as key events. Until that's set up, "no enquiries" is a guess.

Find out who the visitors are

Visits are only worth something if they come from people who might buy. Google Search Console's Search performance report shows the Google searches your site appears in and gets clicks from, broken down by page and by country.

Read the searches and be honest about them. Say you're a plumber and your busiest page is an article on clearing a blocked drain yourself. The people reading it want to fix the drain without you. They're real visitors, and they were never going to call. The same goes for searches for jobs at your company, for a town you don't work in, or from a country you don't serve.

The fix is pages for the work you actually want, in the places you actually serve, written in the words your customers use. Name the suburbs the way locals say them. Put your prices in rand, or explain plainly how you price.

Open your site on a phone as a stranger would

Look at the top of the page before you scroll. Can a stranger tell what you do and where you do it? Is there a way to contact you right there, or do they have to hunt through the menu? A visitor who can't see the next step can simply press back and pick the next result.

Then check the weight of the page. A visitor on a prepaid bundle pays for every megabyte your page downloads. A homepage built around a background video, a slider and a chat widget costs them money before they've read a word. PageSpeed Insights tests a page on mobile and desktop, and where Google has enough visits on record it shows how fast the page loads for real people, beside its own lab test. Run it on your homepage and on your busiest service page.

Give people a reason to trust you before they reach out

A stranger is deciding whether you're real, local and likely to answer. What helps is specific: photos of your own work, the area you cover, a business name that matches your invoices and your Google Business Profile, and a line that says what happens after they send the form.

The form itself matters too. Ask only for what you'll use. Say next to the form why you want the details and who will see them. If you'd like to send newsletters later, ask for that in its own unticked box, separate from the enquiry. Contact forms collect personal information, so POPIA applies, and the Information Regulator publishes guidance on POPIA, including on direct marketing. That's general information. For the wording on your own site, ask an attorney.

Give every page a clear next step

Each page should ask for the thing that page is about. A geyser replacement page asks for a geyser quote. A page about bathroom renovations offers a site visit. Repeat the call to action further down the page, so someone who has read to the end doesn't have to scroll back up to find it.

If your customers would rather send a WhatsApp than fill in a form, give them a WhatsApp button. A button that opens a chat with a message already filled in, saying which page they came from, means the conversation starts with context instead of "Hi".
